The Honest Clock

You cannot measure another machine's clock. This page lets you prove that to yourself in about thirty seconds — with one slider that changes what is true while leaving every number you can observe exactly where it was. Everything else here is what engineers do once they accept it.

The story

Alba wants to know how far ahead Bruno's clock is. She sends a message asking the time and he answers. Four timestamps get written down: when she sent (T1), when he received (T2), when he replied (T3), when she got the answer (T4). From those four numbers she works out her answer.

Why NTP keeps the fastest sample and throws the rest away

Alba does not measure once. She measures eight times. Network delay has a hard floor — nothing travels faster than light down the fibre — and every source of noise (a queue, a busy CPU, a switch buffer) can only add to it. So the delay distribution has a wall on the left and a long tail to the right.

Since a sample's honest error bar is exactly ±δ/2, the fastest sample is the most trustworthy one — not the average of them all. Press the button a few times and watch which strategy wins.

The story

Alba's clock is a vibrating rock, and her rock runs fast. Left alone she gains a fixed number of microseconds every second — that is drift. Every so often she asks a time server where she stands and applies a correction. This panel runs ten minutes of that loop and draws the result.

The green line is how wrong Alba's clock actually is. The shaded band is ε — the error she is prepared to admit to, which is the network uncertainty from Panel 1 plus whatever drift could have accumulated since her last poll. The band must always contain the line; if it doesn't, the clock is lying about how wrong it is.

🔮 Alba corrects her clock to exactly the right value every 64 seconds, and does nothing else. Her crystal is 20 ppm fast. Over ten minutes, what does the error graph look like?
