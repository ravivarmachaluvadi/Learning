The Ferryman’s Choice

Three lighthouse keepers must agree on one thing tonight: light the beacons, or leave them dark. Half a lit coast is worse than either, so all three must do the same thing. They can only write letters. A ferryman carries every letter and never loses one — but the sea decides how long he takes, and nobody owns a clock. Any one keeper might fall ill and stop writing.
This page lets you be the ferryman. You will find you can keep the matter open forever, while delivering every single letter.

The one observation nobody can make

Ada writes to Bo and waits. Nothing comes back. Below are the only two worlds that could explain it. Bo's letter is on a slow boat, or Bo is dead. Ada sees exactly the same thing in both: an empty mailbox.

World A — Bo is alive, the boat is slow

World B — Bo died before writing

What Ada actually sees

Identical in both worlds. There is no letter Ada can write, no question she can ask, and no instrument she can build that separates these two pictures — because the only difference is how long the future will take, and the future has not happened yet.

Ada’s rule

Ada must eventually do something. Choose how long she waits before she declares Bo dead and carries on without him.

The point

Every value you can put on that slider is a guess about the future. Too short and Ada writes off a living keeper. Too long and Ada is stuck. There is no setting that is right in both worlds, because the two worlds are the same world until the letter arrives.

Everything else on this page is a consequence of this single fact.

Why the ferryman always wins

Beating one protocol proves nothing about the next one. The 1985 proof does something stronger: it argues about any protocol at all, using three facts. Each one is shown here on the real keepers, computed live — not asserted.

Fact 1 — “undecided” has a precise meaning: both answers are still reachable

A situation is everything at once: what each keeper knows, plus every letter still at sea. Ask of a situation: from here, which endings are still possible? Click a starting point to see which endings this page can actually produce from it.

Fact 2 — the possibility of one illness is enough. Nobody has to actually fall ill.

Take two starting points that differ only in what Ada wants. Now suppose Ada falls ill before writing a single letter. Bo and Cy must still settle the matter — that is what tolerating one failure means. But their two worlds are identical, so they settle it the same way, whatever Ada wanted.

Fact 3 — two letters to two different islands can be swapped, and nothing notices

This is the lever the whole proof turns on. Pick a situation and two pending events at different islands. Deliver them in either order and compare the resulting situation, keeper by keeper, letter by letter.

How the three facts become the theorem

Fact 1 gives the ferryman a starting position where both endings are still alive.

Fact 3 says the ferryman can reorder anything happening at different islands, so a single island’s next move can be slid earlier or later at will.

Fact 2 closes the last door. Suppose one island’s next move would force the ending. The ferryman lets that island go quiet instead — and by Fact 2 the others must carry on and settle it themselves, without knowing what the quiet island was about to do. Whichever way they go, the ferryman could have gone the other way.

So from a position where both endings are alive, there is always a next event that leaves both endings still alive. Repeat forever, always taking the longest-waiting letter next so nothing is starved. Every letter arrives. Every keeper keeps working. Nobody ever decides.

Honest limits of this page

Fact 3 and Fact 2 are computed here on the actual keepers, and the results are real. Fact 1 is shown by exhibiting one schedule per ending — that proves both endings are reachable, which is what is claimed.

What this page cannot do is check every protocol. The 1985 paper does that with pen and paper, using exactly these three facts and no others.

The four escapes

The theorem is a sentence with load-bearing words in it: no deterministic protocol in an asynchronous system that tolerates one crash can guarantee it always terminates. Every real system escapes by breaking one of those words. Turn each one on and watch what happens to the ferryman.

Which word each escape breaks

Δ
Raft, Paxos, etcd, ZooKeeper		 “asynchronous”. Timeouts stop being guesses and become evidence — but only while the network behaves. Safety never depended on this; liveness always did.
random waits
Raft’s randomised election timeout		 “deterministic” — but weakly. It cures repeated collisions between keepers. It does not cure an unbounded delay, and the table shows it.
random choices
Ben-Or 1983 — not simulated here		 “deterministic” properly: keepers flip coins over the value. Finishes with probability 1 and needs no timing assumption at all. The bad run still exists; it just has probability zero.
oracle
Chandra–Toueg failure detectors		 “no failure information”. Give the keepers a source of hints about who is dead and consensus becomes solvable. The catch: you cannot build that oracle in a truly asynchronous world — in practice you build it out of timeouts, which is escape 1 wearing a hat.

What nobody escapes

None of these make the theorem false. They change the world the theorem is about. In the real world your delivery deadline Δ is a hope, not a guarantee — a long garbage-collection pause, a saturated link, a busy disk, and your cluster is briefly back in the world of tab 2, where nothing finishes.

That is not a bug in your cluster. It is the theorem, arriving on schedule.
