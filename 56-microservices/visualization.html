Splitting the code is not splitting the system

Riverbend split one program into four. The question this tab answers: does that let a team change its own thing without breaking anyone? The answer depends entirely on one decision that has nothing to do with the code — who is allowed to open the storeroom.

Availability multiplies downward

Every service on the path from the customer to the answer must be working. If they are independent, the chance they all work is the product of their individual chances. Everyone's gut says "they're all 99.9%, so we're about 99.9%". Move the sliders and watch what the gut is wrong by.

Two seconds from a slow dependency to a dead service

orders handles 100 checkouts a second and 20 order-history lookups a second. It has a fixed number of worker threads, and a request holds one for its whole duration — including the time it spends waiting for a reply. That single fact is the entire tab. A p99 does not survive being fanned out

A dependency that is slow for 1 request in 100 sounds harmless. But if answering one customer request means waiting for many such dependencies, you wait for the slowest of them — and "1 in 100" stops being rare very quickly.

Your boundaries are measurable

There is one architectural number worth collecting and almost nobody collects it: for the last ten features you shipped, how many separately deployed pieces did each one have to change?
