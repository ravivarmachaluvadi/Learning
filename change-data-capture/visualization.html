The Drawer and the Roll

A shop can be watched two ways: look in the cash drawer every few minutes, or read the till roll that prints every sale as it happens. Databases are the same, and one of the two ways silently loses data. This page lets you run both and count what each one saw.

Meera's grocery app keeps its orders in one database table. Two programs are trying to keep a search index up to date with it. The poller re-reads the table every few seconds and looks for rows with a recent updated_at. The log reader reads the database's own write-ahead log — the list of every change, in the order the database made it real. Both start from the same seeded copy of today's two orders, so from here on they only differ in what they notice. Run a scenario and watch the two indexes drift apart.

What just happened, and why it matters

    The poller here uses the common shape WHERE updated_at > :high_water and sets its high-water mark to the clock at poll time. The variant that instead sets it to max(updated_at) of the rows returned has the same skip, whenever any other row in the batch carries a later stamp.

    The log is not a clean list of finished work. It holds records from transactions that are still open and from transactions that will be rolled back, interleaved in the order the statements ran — not the order they committed. This tab steps a decoder through the raw log record by record. v1 publishes each row change the moment it decodes it. v2 buffers per transaction and publishes only at COMMIT.       What just happened, and why it matters

        To promise the reader "nothing will be missing", the database must refuse to delete log the reader has not read yet. That promise is called a replication slot, and it has no expiry date. A reader that stops reading does not get skipped — it holds log forever, and the log lives on the production database's disk. This is how a broken search-index feeder takes down a payments database.

        What just happened, and why it matters

          Every number on this page is produced by the simulation running in your browser, not measured against a real database. The behaviours modelled — the high-water-mark skip, commit-order buffering, the snapshot handover, slot retention and heartbeats — follow PostgreSQL and Debezium documentation. Real throughput, real latencies and real WAL volumes depend on your schema and traffic; measure your own.
