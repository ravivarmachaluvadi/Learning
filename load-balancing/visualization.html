The only thing that changes is which server each request is sent to, and that turns out to be worth 4× your tail latency.

Predict before you touch anything: at 50% load, how much does the rule matter?

Set the load slider to 0.50 and flip between all four rules. Watch the p99 at the top.

They land in a 16 ms band — 383, 371, 370 and 367 ms — every one of them within 4% of a theoretically perfect balancer. At half load the choice is worth nothing.

Predict: with every request costing exactly the same, how close can round robin get?

Set the spread slider to its lowest setting and race round robin against asking all eight. Most people expect round robin to lose by a little.

It ties exactly — 99.7 ms against 99.7 ms in the ten-minute measurement. When every request costs the same, sending every eighth request to a server is identical to that server taking the front of one shared line. Round robin is not an approximation of the ideal; under those conditions it is the ideal.

Now drag the spread to the top. Round robin goes to 3,863 ms and pure random goes to 3,847 ms — taking turns has become slightly worse than not thinking at all. Round robin was never an algorithm with a quality. It is a bet that all requests cost the same, and it pays out exactly as far as that is true.

What the dispatcher believes

A real load balancer does not have a live feed of every queue. It has statistics, and statistics arrive on a schedule — a metrics scrape, a gossip round, a load header on a health check. Here is what that costs.

Dispatch rule

How old the information is

0 ms is a live feed and does not exist. A Kubernetes metrics scrape is 15–60 seconds. Even a health check carrying a load header is typically 1–10 seconds.

Measure the whole curve

Both rules, staleness from 0 to 1 second. Five simulated minutes and three seeds per point — about 40 seconds, because a herding balancer builds queues hundreds deep and those are expensive to simulate.

Predict: how stale does the information have to be before asking all eight is worse than random?

Asking all eight servers and picking the shortest queue is the best rule in this lab when its information is live — 94 ms against a theoretical floor of 91.5.

At 100 ms of staleness it is worse than choosing at random. At 250 ms it is 2.9× worse than random. At one second it is 9.6× worse.

Watch the two rows of bars below. The top row is what is true. The bottom row is what the dispatcher believes. Every arrival in the gap between refreshes pours into whichever server was empty at the last snapshot — long after it stopped being empty. Then the snapshot updates and the whole stream swings onto the next victim. The servers take turns being crushed, and the fleet's average utilisation never moves, which is why no dashboard shows this.

Dispatch rule

Both failures, all four rules

Five rules × two faults. Traffic shares settle much faster than percentiles do, so this runs four simulated minutes and two seeds per point — within 0.2 percentage points of the document's ten-minute figures. About 5 seconds.

Predict: server A returns errors in 1 ms instead of working. How much traffic does it get?

Eight servers, one of them broken. A fair share is 12.5%. Commit to a number for "ask all eight, pick the shortest" before you run it.

It gets 66.1%. And the cleverest rule on the page — route to whichever server has been answering fastest — sends it 100.0%. Round robin, which knows nothing, gives it exactly 12.5% and is the safest thing here.

The reason is uncomfortable. Every load-aware rule measures how quickly a server gets rid of requests. Failing is the fastest possible way to get rid of a request. A rule that seeks speed will seek failure.

Now switch the fault to "five times slower" and run the same four rules. The order reverses completely: round robin hands a machine that can do 10 requests a second exactly 40 of them, and its queue grows without bound on a fleet running at 80%. No dispatch rule can tell broken from fast. That is what health checking is for, and it is a separate mechanism for exactly this reason.

What this is. A discrete-event simulator of eight backends, each with four workers and an 80 ms mean service time, and a dispatcher whose view of them is configurably imperfect. It is the same engine that produced every number in README.md, and it was checked against the cases where queueing theory knows the exact answer (M/M/1 at ρ = 0.8: 398.6 ms against a theoretical 400; M/M/4 pooled at ρ = 0.8: 139.8 against 140).

What it is not. It models dispatch and queueing only — no TCP, no TLS, no connection setup, and no allowance for the fact that a real server gets slower, not merely busier, as its queue grows. Every one of those makes reality worse than what you see here, never better.

How the sweeps relate to the document. The live panels show a rolling window of the last few thousand requests and will scatter. The sweep buttons run five simulated minutes and three seeds per point, so that a press returns in seconds; README.md uses ten minutes and six seeds. The two agree within about 1% everywhere except very close to saturation (ρ = 0.95), where a nearly-full queue is genuinely noisy and the document says so. The engine itself is identical: given the document's run length and seed count it reproduces every figure in it exactly.
