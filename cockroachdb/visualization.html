CockroachDB · how a database works without an atomic clock

The Reader Pays

Three machines in three cities. Each keeps its own cheap clock, and no two agree. Every write is stamped with the clock of whichever machine happened to handle it — so a write that really happened first can carry a later number than one that happened after it. Google's Spanner fixes this by buying atomic clocks and making every writer wait. CockroachDB buys nothing, and instead makes the reader say "a value stamped anywhere in the next half second might secretly be in my past — I'd better look again." The rules this simulator actually follows

  • Three nodes, five ranges. Mumbai, Frankfurt and Ohio each hold a copy of every range; one of the three is that range's leaseholder and answers its reads without asking the others.
  • Clocks. A node's hybrid-logical clock reads true time + that node's offset. The offsets are the sliders. No node can see its own offset — only this simulator can.
  • Timestamps. A transaction takes its read timestamp from the clock of the node the client connected to. Its commit timestamp starts equal to that and only ever moves up.
  • Doubt. A reader treats any committed value stamped in (read_ts, read_ts + max_offset] as possibly-in-my-past and restarts above it — unless it has already spoken to that value's node at a clock reading below the value's timestamp, in which case the value is provably in the reader's future and is skipped.
  • Latency. Reaching agreement on any range costs one cross-region round trip (the slider, default 60 ms), because a majority always needs a second region. Reading from a leaseholder in your own city costs 1 ms.
  • What is faked. There is no real Raft here, no storage engine, no SQL parser and no network. Every trace on this page is computed from the rules above, and they are a deliberate simplification of the real thing — the lab's write-up says exactly which corners are cut.
